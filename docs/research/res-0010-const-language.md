## 1. The readability-grade ceiling and its instrument ([requirement A10.1](../requirements.md#a10-1))

- a1. **What a grade is.** Every formula in use estimates years of schooling from surface counts: Flesch–Kincaid and the Flesch reading ease from words per sentence and syllables per word. The Gunning fog from words per sentence and the share of "complex" words (three syllables or more, common suffixes removed). SMOG from polysyllables per thirty sentences. The Automated Readability Index and Coleman–Liau from characters per word and words per sentence. Dale–Chall from the share of words outside a list of about three thousand familiar ones. LIX from long words (t2, metric page, and t3's rule files, each with its formula in eight lines). Once the counts are fixed, each is arithmetic: deterministic by construction. What varies between tools is the counting — what a word is, where a sentence ends, how syllables are counted, which word list Dale–Chall consults — which E4 and E5 show.
- a2. **The tools agree on the ranking, not on the number.** On the red fixture Vale reports Flesch–Kincaid 28.10, fog 30.30, Coleman–Liau 21.16, ARI 29.25, SMOG 26.33, Dale–Chall 15.03; textstat on the same text with markup stripped by hand gives 27.45, 32.25, 21.10, 29.84, 26.16 and 12.57; text-readability, Node, gives 34.10 for Flesch–Kincaid on the raw file (E5). Every tool puts the red fixture far above any ceiling and the green one far below; the one-to-three-grade gaps between tools come from the counting, and the Dale–Chall gap of 2.5 grades from the word list. A ceiling therefore belongs to one tool and one formula, named with the number: "grade 10 by Vale's Flesch–Kincaid" is checkable; "grade 10" alone is not.
- a3. **Which formula.** Flesch–Kincaid uses no word list and no suffix rules, so its only inputs are the three counts every tool exposes; its deviations between tools were the smallest in E5. Gunning fog and SMOG depend on a "complex word" definition that differs per tool (Vale's `complex_words` removes `es`, `ed`, `ing` suffixes; textstat's does not, hence 30.30 against 32.25). Dale–Chall depends on a word list, and Vale's page marks SMOG as "calibrated for health writing". Averaging several, which Vale's `readability` check offers, gives a steadier number that no reader can reproduce by hand. One formula, Flesch–Kincaid, is the one a teaching message can explain — "words per sentence and syllables per word" — and the one that public tools (t10) show by default.
- a4. **What Vale measures it on.** The `readability` and `metric` checks "measure the whole document unless it declares a scope", and by default they score the `summary` scope: "The document's prose as one block, excluding headings, code spans, code blocks, and table cells" (t2, scopes page); list items and blockquotes are in. Since 3.21.0 a scope narrows this to a section or a paragraph, and since 3.23.0 the eight scores are built-in variables of `metric` (`flesch_kincaid`, `gunning_fog`, `coleman_liau`, `smog`, `automated_readability`, `dale_chall`, `lix`, `flesch_reading_ease`), so a rule is four lines and needs no package (t2, metric page). E4 shows the exclusion working: the Markdown-handling fixture, whose code fence and URL would read as grade 25 if counted (textstat on the raw file), scores 4.59.
- a5. **The alternatives.** A: Vale's `readability` extension point, one rule with `grade` and `metrics`; the score reported at line 1 of the document; level `error` makes it a red. B: the Readability package (t3), eight rules at `warning` with fixed ceilings — Flesch–Kincaid 8, fog 10, Coleman–Liau 9, ARI 8, SMOG 10, LIX 35, reading ease 70 — pinned by release URL and installed by `vale sync`; the ceilings are the package's, changed only by overriding the rule's level or forking. C: textstat in the gate's own Python, on text the gate strips itself: the formulas match, but the stripping is the module's to write and to get wrong (E5: raw-versus-stripped gaps of 20 grades), and it adds a library to `requirements.txt` for a number Vale already computes. D: Harper's `LongSentences` and no grade: Harper has no readability grade (E4). E: LanguageTool: grammar and style rules, no grade, Java (t7, read, not run). F: an LLM judgment: not deterministic, section 5.
- a6. **A sensible ceiling, by the public guidance.** GOV.UK writes for a reading age of nine and asks to "split up sentences that are over 25 words" (t14); the US federal guide says "Only write for 8th graders if your audience is, in fact, an 8th-grade class" and otherwise for the audience's own level (t14); the Commission's booklet gives "1 sentence = 20 words on average" (t14). None sets a grade for technical documents read by engineers. The kit's own tree, the only consumer today, reads at a Flesch–Kincaid median of 9.33 across 109 documents, from 3.29 to 15.86, with 38 documents above 10, 12 above 12 and 4 above 13 (E2); the kernel reads at 12.07 and `CLAUDE.md` at 13.22. A ceiling of 8, the package's default, would turn 81 of 109 documents red on day one; 10 turns 38; 12 turns 12. The row says "thresholds → binding": the number is a dial, and the module's default is the one number that a consumer with no calibration gets.
- a7. **The reading of the row's "plain English for non-native readers".** A grade ceiling measures syllables and sentence length, not vocabulary choice or idiom; the row's aim is served by the ceiling only in part, and the rest — common words, no idiom, terms defined — is the construction rules of section 2 and the advisory of section 5. The module's document should say so, so that a green Vale run is not read as "plain".

Mechanism class: a formula metric, deterministic once the counts are fixed. Maturity: the formulas are decades old; Vale's built-in scores are new in 3.23.0, its `readability` check older. Cost: one rule file; the binary's cost is section 3's.

**Recommendation: CONFIRMED as a gate on Vale — one `readability` rule of the module's own, Flesch–Kincaid alone, over the document's prose at Vale's default `summary` scope, its `grade` the dial's value, level `error`; the teaching message naming the two counts that move the grade and the fix, shorter sentences and shorter words; the Readability package not used, since its eight ceilings are fixed and its rules duplicate what four lines express.** For the decider: the default ceiling — 10, which turns 38 of the kit's 109 documents red today, or 12, which turns 12; and whether the kit's own dial is set higher than the module's default until its documents are rewritten.

## 2. Sentence-length and construction rules ([requirement A10.1](../requirements.md#a10-1))

- b1. **Sentence length in words, per sentence, in Vale.** A `metric` rule with `scope: sentence` and the formula `words` measures each sentence and reports at that sentence (t2, metric page: scoped `metric` rules need 3.21.0 or later); E1 shows `red.md:10:1:Kit.SentenceLength:Sentence of 46.00 words` and the list item of the Markdown fixture at 64 words, while the code fence, the code span, the long URL and the table cells produced no sentence (E4). The message's `%s` prints two decimals even under `round(words)` (E4), a cosmetic limit the message wording can absorb ("46.00 words" reads as 46). A heading counts as a sentence — the 34-word heading of the front-matter fixture was reported — and a front-matter field does not (E4), so a long `title` field escapes this rule while write-good's rules do reach it.
- b2. **Sentence length elsewhere.** textlint-rule-sentence-length counts characters, not words, in its default: the green fixture, nine sentences of 11 words or fewer, fails it with 9 problems at `max: 25` (E4); textlint-rule-en-max-word-count counts words and agrees with Vale within two words per sentence (44 against 46, 63 against 64), and both textlint rules read the code span at line 20 of the Markdown fixture into the sentence, so a long flag in code font is a long sentence to them. Harper's `LongSentences` reports "This sentence is 46 words long" and skips code and URLs, and its threshold is not a setting: `harper-cli config` lists the rule with a boolean alone (E4). proselint, write-good and alex carry no length rule.
- b3. **The limit, by the public guidance.** GOV.UK: split sentences "over 25 words"; the Commission: 20 on average; Google and Microsoft: "shorter sentences", "short, simple sentences", with no number (t14). Vale's own agents page phrases a writing constraint as "sentences under 25 words" (t2, agents page). On the kit's tree, 2,890 of 9,598 sentences exceed 25 words, 1,189 exceed 40 and 66 exceed 100, the longest 261; the mean is 18.7 (E2). A limit of 25 is the published number and the one that makes a third of the kit's sentences red; 40 makes an eighth red. The number is a dial; the default is the decider's.
- b4. **Forbidden constructions: what is deterministic.** A token list is: `existence` with `tokens` compiles to one word-bounded regular expression, `substitution` maps `observed: expected` pairs and puts the fix in the message (t2, existence and substitution pages; E1 shows `'in order to' is a construction the plain-language rule forbids` and `Use 'before' instead of 'prior to'`). Passive voice by pattern is: write-good's `Passive.yml` matches a form of "be" followed by a past participle from a list, and on a sample of twelve of its 2,227 hits on the kit every one is a real passive — "is declared", "is named", "was committed" (E2); what it cannot tell is whether the passive was chosen, which on the kit it mostly was, so passive is a count of a register, not a defect. Nominalisations by suffix (`-tion`, `-ment`, `-isation`) are a regular expression with many false hits ("documentation", "requirement"); write-good's `TooWordy` list shows the risk: 727 hits on the kit, of which 334 are the word "requirement" and 227 "it is" (E2). Jargon and idiom are lists a consumer writes, since they are the project's; the module can ship the short public lists — the hedges and weasels of write-good's `Weasel.yml` (49 hits on the kit, 39 of them "several"), the redundancies of proselint — as a starting point, each with its own level.
- b5. **What each tool ships.** Vale packages: write-good (passive, weasel, wordy, clichés, there-is, so, e-prime), proselint (cliché, redundancy, jargon, typography and more, 4 hits on the red fixture), alex (insensitive terms, 1 hit on the red fixture for "basically"), Joblint (job-advert phrases), Google and Microsoft (each house's rules: units, ordinals, headings, first person), Std (a base style), Voices (writing voices as rules, requiring 3.20.0), Prompts (rules for instruction files, requiring 3.21.0) (t4; t2, agents page). textlint: the same write-good list as a rule, terminology pairs, comma counts, alex. Harper: 892 grammar rules — spelling, agreement, capitalisation, spacing, redundancy — where the kit's tree yields 224 spelling hits on one document for its handles `A5`, `M6`, `dec` (E4); no configurable thresholds, no readability grade beyond `LongSentences`, and "Machine-readable output" is listed in its readme as a possible future feature (t8). LanguageTool: grammar and style rules in Java with a server and a public HTTP API (t7). retext: readability per sentence by a vote of seven formulas, passive, simplify, over raw text unless a Markdown parser is put in front (E5).
- b6. **False positives are the cost of a gate on prose.** Every construction rule fires on the documents that quote the construction — this comparison names `in order to`, which fired its own rule until the phrase went into a code span — and on the register a project chose. Vale's answer is per-rule levels (`error` blocks, `warning` and `suggestion` report), in-file comments `<!-- vale Style.Rule = NO -->`, `exceptions` on a rule, and path-keyed sections of the configuration (t2, Markdown and BlockIgnores pages); the kit's answer for its other scans is a negated territory pattern and, for the style scan, a declared departure with a reason. A gate here needs the same escape: a territory the consumer narrows, and a per-rule level the consumer lowers, not a comment in the document — the kit's style guide says a departure is declared in the dials file, "never in the document itself".

Mechanism class: a rule-based linter — a scoped metric for length, token lists for constructions, a pattern for passive. Maturity: Vale's scoped `metric` is a year old at most (3.21.0), its `existence` and `substitution` a decade; the public lists are stable and rarely released. Cost: rule files of a few lines each; the fixtures of [law 7 of CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md#law-7), one per rule.

**Recommendation: CONFIRMED as a gate on Vale — a `metric` rule at `scope: sentence` with the word limit as a dial, level `error`; the module's own short token lists as `existence` and `substitution` rules, shipped with the plugin and written for a reader whose first language is not English, level `error`; passive voice reported at `warning`, never a red, since on the kit it is a register, and a dial to raise it; no third-party package installed by the gate.** For the decider: the default word limit, 25 by the published guidance or 40 by what the kit's own prose can bear today; and whether the consumer extends the lists through the dials file, which asks the gate to generate rule files (section 3), or through a style of its own beside the module's.

## 3. Packaging, pinning and who installs the binary ([requirement A10.1](../requirements.md#a10-1), [law 2 of CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md#law-2))

- c1. **The binary is not a library.** Vale is a Go binary of about 12 MB per platform, released for Linux, macOS and Windows on two architectures with a checksum file (E1); it is installed by `brew install vale`, `mise use vale@3.23.0`, `npm install --save-dev @vvago/vale`, `pip install vale`, conda, apt, pacman, snap, the release archive, or `go install` (t2, installation page). The npm and PyPI packages are wrappers that download the same release binary; the PyPI one is a third party's — its metadata says the author "is not affiliated nor endorsed" — pinned at 3.22.0.0 on the survey date and fetching from the old organisation's release URL, which redirects today (t9, read). Every kit gate so far needs only `python3` and PyYAML ([dec-0015-pre-module-audit-rulings](../decisions/dec-0015-pre-module-audit-rulings.md)); this is the first that needs a binary on `PATH`, and the template's dependency rule speaks of `requirements.txt` and one `pip` command ([template §6](../../module-template/MODULE.md#t6), rule 7), which a binary does not fit.
- c2. **Who installs it.** A: the consumer, by the package manager of its platform, and the gate's configuration failure names the command — `brew install vale` on macOS, the installation page's address for the rest — and refuses to run until it is there: the kit's rule, "a missing interpreter or library is a configuration failure: exit 1 with the install command in the message, never a silent pass" (step 4 of "Developing a module" in [CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md)). B: the plugin ships the six binaries — 74 MB in a plugin whose other modules weigh kilobytes, an update per Vale release, and a licence file to carry; nothing in the platform's plugin loading installs a binary, only Node packages from a lockfile (t1, plugin loading page). C: the gate downloads the release into the plugin's data directory at first run, checksum verified — the pattern the platform documents for `node_modules` in `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_DATA}` (t1, plugin components page) — a network act inside a hook, a directory that exists only when the plugin runs as a plugin (a scan in CI has no `CLAUDE_PLUGIN_DATA`), and a second copy per machine. D: the PyPI wrapper as a declared library: a third party's package, a network fetch at first run, one version behind. Vale's own Claude Code plugin chose A and says so: "You also need the `vale` binary on your `PATH` … the plugin drives it, it doesn't bundle it" (t12, readme).
- c3. **The version floor.** A scoped `metric` needs 3.21.0, the built-in scores 3.23.0, and Homebrew ships 3.23.0 on the survey date (t2, installation page; the formula's entry read today). The gate reads `vale --version` at the preflight and treats an older binary as a configuration failure with the floor in the message, so a rule that silently never fires on an old Vale cannot pass a write. E1 recorded `vale version 3.23.0`.
- c4. **Where the rules live.** A package is "a style, a configuration file, or both, published as an archive", installed by `vale sync` into `StylesPath`, pinned by giving "the release URL in place of the name" (t2, packages page); E1 pinned three packages that way, and `vale sync --plain-progress` installed them in one run. But the module's rules of sections 1 and 2 are its own YAML files, and a `StylesPath` may be any directory: the plugin ships them under its own root and the gate names that directory through `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}` — no `sync`, no network, no `StylesPath` in the consumer's tree, nothing to pin. The rules the module ships are versioned with the plugin, which is the kit's own pinning (step 7 of "Developing a module" in [CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md)). A consumer that wants Google, Microsoft or Voices writes its own `.vale.ini` with `Packages` pinned by release URL, and that file is the consumer's, outside the module — the specification's "pin packages by release URL" then describes the consumer's route, not the module's.
- c5. **Thresholds in the binding.** A Vale rule's `grade`, `condition` and `level` are fields of a YAML file in the style directory, not of the consumer's dials file. Two ways to make the dial rule: A, the gate reads its namespace — the grade ceiling, the word limit, the levels, the extra tokens — and writes a configuration and a style into a temporary directory on each run, then runs `vale --config=<that file> --no-global --output=JSON <file>`; the rule files in the plugin become templates, and the dials file is the one calibration surface, as [kernel §6](../../kernel/CONVENTIONS.md#k6) asks ("install-and-run must work with zero configuration", defaults when the namespace is absent). B, the consumer edits a `.vale.ini` and rule files in its tree, and the module's dial names only the territories — cheaper to build, but the thresholds then live outside the dials file, against the row's "thresholds → binding", and two calibration surfaces exist. A is the shape that keeps one surface, at the price of a generator step inside the gate; the generated files are a handful of lines (E1's four rules and one `.vale.ini`).
- c6. **Isolation.** Vale reads "the user-level configuration in the operating system's config directory … underneath" the project's, unless `--no-global` is given, and finds `.vale.ini` "in the working directory or any directory above it, then in the home directory" (t2, CLI page). A gate that must give one verdict per repository passes `--config` and `--no-global` always, so a developer's home configuration cannot change what CI sees.
- c7. **Portability test.** With A of c5, the module on an empty repository with the kernel alone runs with its defaults: no dials file, no `.vale.ini`, no `StylesPath` in the tree; the only external need is the binary, refused loudly when absent. No path of a project enters the module ([requirement A12.1](../requirements.md#a12-1)); the generated configuration lives outside the tree.

Mechanism class: packaging. Maturity: the release channel and the package managers are stable; the generated-configuration shape is the module's own. Cost: one binary the consumer installs; a generator of a few lines in the gate; a preflight of the version.

**Recommendation: the consumer installs the binary (A of c2), and the gate's configuration failure names `brew install vale` and the installation page and refuses on a version below 3.23.0; the module ships its rules inside the plugin, reached through `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}`, and generates the Vale configuration from its dials on each run (A of c5), with `--config` and `--no-global`; no package is synced by the gate and nothing is pinned by URL inside the module — the specification's pinning line becomes the consumer's route for its own packages. CONFIRMED that the mechanism is Vale; the specification's "ships Vale config" is refined to "ships rules and generates the config".** For the decider: A or B of c5; and whether an extra style of the consumer's, named in a dial by path, is admitted at founding.

## 4. The enforcement moment ([requirement A10.1](../requirements.md#a10-1))

- d1. **What the matrix says and what exists.** "Vale in CI, red = fix"; the kit has no CI on the survey date, and its gate list runs by the `gate-list-runner` agent before a commit and a merge ([CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md), "Session conduct"). Every document module of the kit ships a `--scan` for that list, and CI when it comes will run the same scans (the pattern of every shipped module; [kit-shared-gate-code §2](../../shared/GATE.md#g2)).
- d2. **A hook per file is fast enough.** Vale lints the kit's largest document, 65,584 characters, in 0.18 s, the requirements document in 0.09 s, a 7,053-character page in 0.04 s, and the whole tree of 115 files in 1.06 s; a Python wrapper that runs the binary and parses its JSON takes 0.24 s wall for the requirements document, 0.095 s of it inside Vale (E7). The platform's default hook timeout is 600 s (t1, hooks page); the kit's gates declare 30. textlint took 6.92 s for the tree and would fit too; proselint 0.22 s per document.
- d3. **Before or after the write.** The kit's gates run on `PreToolUse` with the pending document reconstructed, so an invalid envelope never reaches disk ([kit-shared-gate-code §1](../../shared/GATE.md#g1), `events`). A prose gate before the write has a cost the envelope gate does not: a document that is red in ten sentences stays red after an `Edit` that fixes one, so a pre-write block refuses every partial improvement and the model must rewrite the whole document in one `Write` to get through — the opposite of the loop a linter should drive. After the write, the platform's `PostToolUse` gives what a linter needs: "`decision`: `"block"` adds the `reason` next to the tool result. Claude still sees the original output", and "exit 2 … Shows stderr to Claude; the tool already ran" (t1, hooks page, decision control and the exit-code table); an `additionalContext` string "wraps the string in a system reminder and inserts it into the conversation at the point where the hook fired", capped at 10,000 characters with the rest written to a file the model is not asked to read (t1, hooks page). Vale's own plugin runs on `PostToolUse` with the matcher `Edit|Write` and hands the alerts back as `additionalContext` (t12, `hooks.json` and `vale-edit.sh`). The shared pipeline already reads "the file on disk" for "an event after the write" ([kit-shared-gate-code §1](../../shared/GATE.md#g1), `events`).
- d4. **What the hook cannot see.** "Claude Code doesn't run a `PostToolUse` hook matching `Edit|Write` when a `Bash` command or a process outside Claude Code rewrites the same file" (t1, hooks page): the shared honest limit of every kit gate ([kit-shared-gate-code §4](../../shared/GATE.md#g4), item 1), backstopped by the scan.
- d5. **Vale's own plugin as the alternative to building.** `vale@agent-tools` ships five skills, an edit-time hook and a paid MCP server (t12). Its hook is "Silent by design": `command -v vale >/dev/null 2>&1 || exit 0`, and "A repository that hasn't adopted Vale, a machine without the binary, a file Vale doesn't lint, or a clean run all exit 0 with no output"; it reports errors only by default, through a `level` user option; it walks up from the file to find a `.vale.ini` and runs Vale there (t12, `vale-edit.sh`). It reads no dials, has no scan, prints Vale's own messages, and its silence on a missing binary is the fail-open that [law 5 of CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md#law-5) and the kit's runtime rule forbid. Its readme's own remark holds: "If nothing happens, that is often correct — the hook is silent unless a file Vale lints has an error." A consumer may install it beside the kit; the kit's gate cannot be it.
- d6. **CI wiring, read for the consumer.** The GitHub Action `vale-cli/vale-action@v3.0.0` takes `version` (default `latest`), `files`, `sync`, `reporter` (four reviewdog modes), `fail_on_error` (default false), `fail_level`, `filter`, `glob`, `min_alert_level`, `config` and `vale_flags` (t12, `action.yml`); it comments on the pull request through reviewdog, and by default does not fail the job. A pre-commit entry pins `rev: v3.23.0` (t2, pre-commit page). The kit's own CI, when it comes, runs the module's `--scan` like every other scan, which needs only the binary on the runner — `brew install vale` or the release archive — and no action.
- d7. **The two moments, priced.** The scan is the gate the matrix names — deterministic, exit 1 on a red, run before a commit, a merge and in CI. The post-write hook is the same check on one file, the teaching moment: with `severity: block` it exits 2 and the model sees the findings beside its tool result and fixes them in the same turn; with `severity: warn`, the adoption path, the findings ride as `additionalContext`. Neither prevents a write, which is honest to say: the row's red is a fix owed before the change lands, not a write refused.

Mechanism class: a deterministic scan plus a post-write hook, both over the same Vale run. Maturity: the scan is the kit's pattern in nine modules; the post-write hook is documented and in use by Vale's plugin. Cost: one hook entry; the shared pipeline's post-write path, unused by a kit gate until now.

**Recommendation: CONFIRMED as a gate, the moment widened — the `--scan` over the module's `territories` is the gate the matrix calls "Vale in CI", red on any `error`-level alert; and a `PostToolUse` hook on `Write|Edit` runs the same check on the written file and returns the findings to the model, exit 2 under `severity: block`, `additionalContext` under `warn`; no `PreToolUse` gate, since a document-wide rule on a pre-write hook refuses every partial fix.** For the decider: whether the hook ships at founding or waits for the first consumer session that asks for it, as the scan alone satisfies the row.

## 5. Plain chat language ([requirement A10.2](../requirements.md#a10-2))

- e1. **Where it is.** Rule I-i of module `const-interaction`, printed at every session start: "Write chat in plain language for a reader whose first language is not English: short sentences, common words, no idiom, a term defined at first use" (the kit's tree at `5a43bec`, `modules/const-interaction/scripts/rules.txt`), advisory, declared "honestly ungateable" by the matrix and by that module's document ([`const-interaction`](../requirements.md#const-interaction), its row line). The matrix's boundary note asks whether the row moves here.
- e2. **What could be measured.** A reply is not a file: no `Write` or `Edit` carries it, so no tool hook sees it. The one hook that runs when a reply is complete is `Stop`, which receives `transcript_path` (t1, hooks page), and a script could read the last assistant message from the transcript and run Vale over it: deterministic, and the shared code has a `Stop` event already ([kit-shared-gate-code §1](../../shared/GATE.md#g1), `events`). But the reply has been shown to the user by then; a block at `Stop` makes the model write a second reply that restates the first, and a reply is not a document: it quotes code, paths and the user's words, and its sentences are the user's questions answered in kind. The transcript's shape is the platform's and not documented as an interface. A measurement is possible; a gate on it would be dressed judgment, which [law 5 of CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md#law-5) forbids.
- e3. **An LLM as judge.** A `prompt` or `agent` hook could rate a paragraph or a reply for plainness (t1, hooks page: hook types `prompt` and `agent` exist with their own timeouts). Two runs on the same text need not agree; the platform's own page says nothing of determinism, and none is claimed here. That is advisory by the kit's law: judgment shaped, never a red. An advisory hook that costs a model call per turn for a rule already printed at session start adds nothing the printed rule does not.
- e4. **The three boundary tests.** One invariant: the same — plain language for the same reader. One enforcement moment: no — a document is checked at its write and in a scan, a reply at no moment. One lifecycle trigger: no — the interaction module enters on reply-shape friction, this one on prose-complexity complaints about documents. Two of three tests keep the row where it is. What this module owes the row is one sentence in its document: the document half of the language aspect is here, the chat half stays advisory in the interaction module, and the two share the reader they name.
- e5. **What a consumer without the interaction module gets.** Nothing for chat, and the kit's sibling rule applies: this module cannot require that module ([law 3 of CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md#law-3)); it may say "when `const-interaction` is installed, its session-start rule carries the chat half".

Mechanism class: advisory prose on a session-start carrier; a `Stop`-time measurement possible and rejected. Maturity: shipped and printed since the interaction module. Cost: none here.

**Recommendation: CONFIRMED as advisory and honestly ungateable; the row stays with module `const-interaction` by two of the three boundary tests, and this module's document names it as the chat half of one aspect; no `Stop`-time measurement and no LLM judge.** For the decider: whether the row's destination in the matrix is settled as the interaction module's now, closing the boundary note's question, or left flagged until a consumer reports the split as confusing.

## 6. Graduation of the styling module's signatures ([requirement A9.4](../requirements.md#a9-4))

- f1. **What the styling module holds.** Twelve signatures in its own scan on the shared gate code — `plantuml`, `formula`, `callout`, `deep-heading`, `skipped-level`, `second-h1`, `fence-language`, `list-marker`, `list-numbering`, `underscore-emphasis`, `table-columns` and `table-empty-cell` — with departures declared per path or pattern in the dials file with a reason, and no hook (the kit's tree at `5a43bec`, `modules/const-styling/scripts/styling_gate/model.py` and [§12 of the styling module's document](../../modules/const-styling/MODULE.md#t12)); its scan is green on the kit today under one departure (E8).
- f2. **What Vale expresses of them.** E6 wrote one `existence` rule at `scope: raw` with one raw regular expression and one `script` rule in Tengo, and ran them on a fixture holding every signature. The regular expression catches the PlantUML fence and the bare `@startuml`, the `$$` line, the `> [!` callout, the level-four heading, the `*` and `+` bullets and the underscore emphasis — six kinds, cleanly — and misfires on every closing fence, since a closing fence and an opening fence with no language are the same three characters to a pattern with no state; `fence-language`, `skipped-level` and `list-numbering` need the state a script keeps. The script caught the short table row and the empty cell in twelve lines. The twelfth, `second-h1`, is a `metric` rule on the variable `heading.h1` with the condition `> 1` (t2, metric page; read, not run). So all twelve are expressible, five of them only as scripts, and the `raw` scope is a condition, as [res-0009-const-styling](res-0009-const-styling.md) found (its e2): at the default scope the fence is invisible. A lookbehind compiled inside the rule's pattern (E6); the script page limits scripts alone to Go's syntax.
- f3. **What Vale cannot express.** The departures dial: a path, the signatures allowed there and a reason in one line. Vale's nearest form is a path-keyed section of the configuration with `Style.Rule = NO` (t2, MinAlertLevel and BlockIgnores pages), which switches a rule off for a path and carries no reason; an in-file comment `<!-- vale Style.Rule = NO -->` marks the document, which the style guide forbids. The generator of section 3 could write the sections from the dial and keep the reason in the dials file — possible, and the twelve rules would then exist twice: once in the styling module's Python, once in this module's YAML, with two fixture sets and two messages per signature, against [law 1 of CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md#law-1)'s spirit if not its letter.
- f4. **What graduation would buy.** One tool where two run; an editor's Vale integration showing the signatures as it types. What it costs: the duplication of f3, a dependency on the binary for rules that today need only `python3`, and an order between two modules that the kit's sibling rule forbids ([law 3 of CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md#law-3)): the styling module cannot drop a rule because this module carries it, since a consumer may install either alone.
- f5. **The reading that costs nothing.** The row says the defaults "graduate to Vale rules when `const-language` installed" — a possibility the styling module records for a rule that a reader of words needs, its deferred item 4: sentence case in headings and a key column first, "both need a reader of words, which the graduation path carries" ([§12 of the styling module's document](../../modules/const-styling/MODULE.md#t12)). Those are the candidates: a rule about words is this module's kind, a rule about markup is the styling scan's. Sentence-case headings are Vale's `capitalization` check at `scope: heading` (t2, scopes page; the Google package's `Headings.yml` is that rule, read in [res-0009-const-styling](res-0009-const-styling.md)). None of the twelve is about words.

Mechanism class: third-party rules duplicating a shipped scan. Maturity: E6 proves the shape; nothing is shipped. Cost: twelve rules, twelve fixtures, a second home per rule.

**Recommendation: not needed — no signature graduates; the styling scan stays the one home of the twelve, and this module's document records E6's shape — one `existence` rule at `scope: raw` for the stateless six, a `metric` on `heading.h1` for the second title, a `script` for the five that need state — so that a future graduation of a rule about words, sentence-case headings first, copies rather than invents; the row's "then the gate of the language row" is read as the path for word rules, and OVERTURNED for the twelve markup signatures.** For the decider: whether sentence-case headings, the styling module's deferred item 4, are this module's first rule at founding.
